AI intake for immigration consultants in Canada: what automation can handle under the CICC Code

An AI intake assistant for an immigration practice answers every inquiry at any hour, sorts it by pathway and urgency, collects the facts a consultant needs and books a paid consultation. Advice on a person's options stays with the licensee. Section 91 of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act and the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants (CICC) Code of Professional Conduct draw that line, and they also shape what the assistant collects before the first consultation.

Published November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

This guide is for Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultants (RCICs) and the intake leads at their practices. It covers the inquiry mix, the legal line between intake and advice, the Code sections that shape an intake flow, and a field list for first contact. It is operational guidance; for a ruling on your own practice, ask the College or your own counsel.

The legal line: intake versus advice

Under section 91 of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, no person may knowingly represent or advise a person for consideration in connection with an application or proceeding under the Act, or offer to do so, unless they are a lawyer or Quebec notary, another member of a provincial law society or the Chambre des notaires (including a paralegal), or a member in good standing of the College.

The CICC Code of Professional Conduct (SOR/2022-128) applies that line inside a practice. Section 38(3) lets a licensee "delegate certain aspects of their work to a person who is not a licensee" while ensuring that person gives no representation or advice in contravention of section 91. Section 38(2) requires the licensee to "supervise and assume professional responsibility" for work done by anyone who assists. The Code text does not name AI tools. Our reading, for intake design: treat the assistant like a non-licensee assistant, give it administrative tasks only, and keep a licensee responsible for what it says. Section 19(2)(e) adds a competence duty to "deliver the services to the client using technology that is appropriate and effective".

What the assistant handles, and what goes to a licensee

The assistant handles the administrative first contact:

  • Reply to forms, email, chat and missed calls at any hour and confirm receipt.
  • Identify the pathway family (work, study, family, business, humanitarian, citizenship) from a few questions.
  • Collect name, contact details, current status in Canada and its expiry date, province, and any pending application with its program and filing date.
  • Ask whether another representative is on the file and record the preferred language.
  • State published facts only: consultation fee, format, office hours, and links to IRCC pages.
  • Send the consultation agreement, book the slot and send reminders.
  • Run follow-up for people who are still deciding.

A licensee handles everything that is advice or judgment:

  • Any answer to "Am I eligible?", "Which program is best for me?" or "What are my chances?"
  • Deadlines set by IRCC or a tribunal, and status that is about to expire.
  • Conflict of interest decisions, which the Code ties to written disclosure and written consent (s. 15).
  • Accepting the client and signing the service agreement.
  • Distressed callers and anything involving safety; in an emergency, the assistant directs the person to 911.

People should also know when they are talking to an automated assistant. Canada's privacy commissioner addressed this in October 2026; see our note on telling clients they are talking to AI.

A first-contact field list for RCIC practices

Ten fields are enough to route an inquiry and prepare a consultation. Each one feeds either the consultation agreement or the consultant's preparation.

  1. Full name, as on the passport.
  2. Email, phone and home address (needed for the consultation agreement under s. 23(2)(b)).
  3. Preferred language for the consultation.
  4. Country of citizenship and country of residence.
  5. Current status in Canada, if any, and its expiry date.
  6. Province or territory of residence or intended destination.
  7. Pathway family the person is asking about, in their own words.
  8. Any pending application: program and the date IRCC received it.
  9. Any date set by IRCC or a tribunal that the person mentions.
  10. Whether another representative is working on the file.

Today, a private practice's intake data falls under PIPEDA in most provinces. According to the Office of the Privacy Commissioner, Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec have their own substantially similar private-sector laws, and PIPEDA applies whenever personal information crosses provincial or national borders, which is routine for immigration files. Passport scans and identity documents belong in the document collection step after the service agreement, with access limited to the case team.

FAQ: AI intake for immigration consultants

Can an AI assistant give immigration advice to a prospective client?

It should route every advice question to a licensee. Section 91 of IRPA limits paid representation and advice to lawyers, Quebec notaries, other law society members such as paralegals, and members of the College, and section 38(3) of the CICC Code says work delegated to a non-licensee must not include such advice.

Does the CICC Code of Professional Conduct mention AI?

The Code text does not name AI tools. Its rules on delegation, supervision, confidentiality, communication and marketing apply to the work the tool does for the licensee, and the licensee supervises and assumes professional responsibility for that work under section 38(2).

What must be in place before an RCIC's first consultation?

Section 23 of the CICC Code requires a written consultation agreement before the initial consultation. It includes the licensee's details, the potential client's name and contact information, the consultation fee, a brief description of the College's role and the purpose and scope of the consultation.

Can an immigration chatbot promise that an application will succeed?

No. Section 44(2) of the CICC Code bars licensees, in marketing, from guaranteeing the success of an application, making false or misleading representations, or implying a relationship with the Government of Canada or a provincial government. Automated replies follow the same rule.
