Telling clients they are talking to AI: what Canada's privacy commissioner told Ottawa in October 2026

On October 23, 2026, the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (OPC) published its submission to the federal consultation on AI transparency. The OPC names two situations where people should know AI is involved: when an automated system makes or informs significant decisions about them, and when an AI system, such as a chatbot, collects or uses their personal information. Any firm that answers web chats, forms or calls with an AI intake assistant falls into the second group.

Published November 9, 2026 · 5 min read

What to do: make the first line of every AI chat or call say that the client is talking to an AI assistant and what it does, explain what information it collects and who reviews it in the same privacy notice you already use, and offer a person on request. Fee quotes, offers and legal or eligibility opinions stay with your staff.

What happened

Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) ran a public consultation on AI transparency from July 23 to September 23, 2026. Its discussion paper covers five areas: AI-generated content, AI interaction, information about AI systems, AI incidents and AI agents. ISED says a What We Heard report will follow in the coming months.

The OPC sent its comments on September 23 and announced them on October 23. The full submission agrees that transparency issues can arise in all five areas and answers ISED's questions on AI interaction in detail.

What the OPC submission says

QuestionOPC position
When should people know they are dealing with AI?When automated systems make or inform significant predictions, recommendations or decisions about them, and when an AI system collects or uses their personal information, including an AI chatbot
What makes a disclosure meaningful?The OPC suggests adapting the four elements of its Guidelines on Obtaining Meaningful Consent: what personal information is collected, who it is shared with, for what purposes, and the risks of harm and other consequences
Where should the disclosure live?Consistent with existing privacy notices; people should not have to read separate documents depending on whether AI is involved
Can there be too much disclosure?Yes. The OPC agrees there is a risk of over-disclosure and supports a balance that does not overwhelm people
What should businesses using AI check?Accuracy limits disclosed by the AI provider; without them, whether the use remains appropriate and legally authorized

The case ISED used to explain the risk

The discussion paper describes a May 2026 case in Toronto. A man asked a dealership to buy back his car through an online inquiry. A text assistant named "Quinn" offered $27,162.79, said the dealership would consider his counteroffer of $28,500 and set a meeting time. A sales consultant then called, said Quinn was an AI chatbot that had made the offer in error, and put the maximum at $20,000. Quinn never indicated it was not a human. The dealership honoured the original offer after the man took the story to the media.

For a law firm, clinic or immigration practice, the same pattern shows up as a chatbot that quotes a fee, promises a result or gives a view on a case. The paper also notes that the federal government's proposed Bill C-34, the Safe Social Media Act, includes rules against chatbots deceptively posing as a human or as a medical, legal or other licensed professional. The House of Commons referred Bill C-34 to committee on October 29, 2026.

Where the law stands in November 2026

The OPC submission is advice to a consultation. Today, PIPEDA already requires that the purposes of collecting personal information be stated so a person can reasonably understand them, and the OPC suggests building AI disclosures on its meaningful consent guidelines. Bill C-36, the proposed Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act, was introduced on June 15, 2026 and is at second reading in the House of Commons. ISED's paper notes that the EU AI Act, Colorado and Maine already require disclosure when people interact with an AI system, and that Ontario requires provincially regulated employers to disclose AI use in hiring.

A six-step check for your AI intake

  1. Open every chat, text thread and AI-answered call with a plain sentence: the client is talking to the firm's AI assistant, and it collects details and books consultations.
  2. List in your privacy notice what the assistant collects, which providers process it and why. Link that notice from the chat window.
  3. Name the role that reviews each conversation and the time within which a person replies.
  4. Offer a person at any point, and route "speak to someone" requests to staff the same day.
  5. Block the assistant from quoting fees, making offers, and giving legal, medical or eligibility opinions. Route those questions to staff.
  6. Ask your AI vendor in writing for known accuracy limits, and keep conversation logs your team can review.

Questions business owners are asking

Do I have to tell customers they are chatting with AI in Canada?

The federal bills in this area are still proposals as of November 2026: Bill C-34 proposes rules against chatbots deceptively posing as a human, and Bill C-36 covers transparency for automated decision systems. PIPEDA already requires purposes for collecting personal information to be explained so people can understand them, and the Office of the Privacy Commissioner says people should know when an AI chatbot collects or uses their personal information.

What did the Privacy Commissioner publish on October 23, 2026?

Its submission to ISED's consultation on AI transparency. It agrees with the five areas ISED identified and suggests that the four elements of its meaningful consent guidelines could be adapted for AI disclosures.
