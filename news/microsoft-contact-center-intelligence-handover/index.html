Microsoft releases Contact Center Intelligence for existing contact centres

Microsoft made Contact Center Intelligence generally available on November 18. The release gives operators a way to add AI to an existing contact centre and hand a conversation back to a representative in the desktop that representative already uses. For a service business reviewing an AI intake proposal, it puts a specific part of the demo under the spotlight. Follow one customer from the automated greeting all the way to the person who can act on the request.

November 21, 2026

What the Microsoft release covers

In its general availability announcement, Microsoft describes connecting the product to an existing contact-centre platform. Agents built there can handle self-service over voice and digital channels. Microsoft also announced partnerships with Avaya, Cisco and Five9. An operator considering a particular setup should check which connection is available for its own platform and contract.

The announcement describes a built-in quality assurance agent that can score and summarise interactions. A score can help an operations manager choose a conversation for review. To judge the quality of intake itself, read what the next employee actually received. A polished summary that omits the customer's return number leaves the desk with an attractive dead end.

Inspect the handover on the receiving side

Take a fictional repair business. A customer phones about a washing machine that was repaired last week. They have a job reference but cannot remember the technician's name. The sales rep who quotes for a first visit has nothing to sell here. Put the reference in front of the service coordinator. That colleague can reopen the repair and find the name on last week’s worksheet.

Ask the demonstrator to change seats. View the interaction as the coordinator would see it first thing in the morning. Look for the link to last week’s visit. Look for the note that says the caller can pick up only after lunch. Somebody who missed the original conversation should be able to decide what to do next without replaying the whole recording.

  1. Enter an ordinary new enquiry in the demonstration environment. Note the queue and the role that owns it.
  2. Introduce an existing job reference midway through the conversation. See whether the destination changes and whether the original request survives the move.
  3. Have the usual recipient go off duty. Watch where the handover lands and how a supervisor discovers that it is waiting.
  4. Ask the receiving employee to mark the follow-up complete. Check what the originating desk sees after that happens.

Decide which evidence justifies the purchase

For a supplier demo, use fictional customer details and agree which staff member will judge the result. One manager may care about abandoned calls while a service coordinator is losing time on duplicate tickets. Both problems can show up in the same conversation. Write the expected result in operational terms, such as “the old job is reopened once and the coordinator has the callback window.”

Show that result to whoever owns the service contract. Ask which part depends on the contact-centre platform and which part depends on work in your booking system. If a separate company manages the calendar, find out whether that supplier has included its part in the quote. Otherwise a successful transfer on screen can mask an expensive manual re-entry at the next desk.

Start with the channel your customers already use

A small service firm may have a shared email inbox and a missed-call list in place of a staffed contact centre. The same test still applies. The morning coordinator needs enough context to tell a new lead from a returning customer and a clear signal that someone is expecting a reply.
