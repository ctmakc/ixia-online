Microsoft releases Contact Center Intelligence for existing contact centres

Microsoft made Contact Center Intelligence (CCI) generally available on November 18, 2026, a month after the public preview opened in October. Omar Aftab, Microsoft's vice president for conversational AI and contact centre AI, announced it the same day Microsoft ran an Ignite session titled "Modernize your contact center without rip and replace." CCI places Microsoft-built AI agents in front of a contact centre a company already runs. The agents use the existing telephony and routing, and when a caller needs a person they transfer the conversation to a representative who stays in the desktop software they already use.

November 21, 2026

What the Microsoft release covers

According to the general availability announcement, an open connector already links CCI to any contact centre platform, and Microsoft has partnered with Avaya, Cisco and Five9 to build and maintain ready-made connectors for theirs. Which connector a particular operator gets depends on its platform and its contract.

Agents can work in teams, with each sub-agent holding its own knowledge and tools, and a custom agent written in Microsoft Foundry can join the team. Where a conversation has to run the same way every time, as in a long IVR menu, the builder allows scripted turn-by-turn dialogs. Voice settings include DTMF handling, speech sensitivity and messages that tell a caller the agent is still working on something. Microsoft also says CCI will offer Microsoft AI Realtime 1, its first speech-to-speech model.

Callers can be verified by voiceprint while they explain the problem, and a caller whose line drops can be re-authenticated when they ring back and pick up where they left off. A Quality Assurance Agent scores and summarises each interaction. On the transfer itself Microsoft's wording is short: context and voice authentication carry over when a call moves to a representative.

A QA score helps a manager pick calls to review. Whether the next desk received the customer's job number is a separate check, and a polished summary without that number still sends somebody back to the phone.

Inspect the handover on the receiving side

At a repair company, a customer calls about a washing machine repaired last week. They have the job reference and have forgotten the technician's name. The call belongs with the service coordinator, who reopens the job and finds the name on last week's worksheet, and the handover note is useful if it carries the job number, the appliance, the hours the customer can answer and their own words about the fault. In a demo, the coordinator's screen at 8 a.m. is the one to watch:

  1. An ordinary new enquiry: which queue and role it lands with.
  2. An existing job reference mentioned halfway through: whether the call is rerouted and the original request survives the move.
  3. The usual recipient off shift: where the handover waits and how a supervisor finds it.
  4. The follow-up marked complete: what the originating desk sees afterwards.

Decide which evidence justifies the purchase

Demo calls use fictional customer details. The service coordinator, who cleans up duplicate tickets, judges the result along with the operations manager, who watches abandoned calls. A one-line target set beforehand ("old job reopened once, callback window on screen") keeps the test concrete. When the booking calendar runs on another vendor's product, that vendor's share of the integration belongs in the quote too.

Start with the channel your customers already use

Many small service firms work from a shared inbox, a web form and a missed-call list. The same test applies: the morning coordinator tells a new lead from a returning customer and sees who is waiting for a reply.
